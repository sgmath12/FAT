\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

This section investigates the use of a naturally trained model to mitigate the loss of clean accuracy
that adversarial training incurs.
Most existing methods transfer the clean accuracy of such a model through knowledge distillation,
matching its logits alongside the label loss \citep{lbgat, arrest, dpfat}.
More recent work argues that a one-hot target is too sharp for adversarial training and replaces it
with a softened teacher distribution \citep{ard, rslad, LTD, adr}, and a naturally trained model
represents the extreme of that spectrum, since its logits are sharper still.
We revisit this softened-logit route more rigorously, and find that softening improves robustness
while leaving the clean-accuracy gap almost unchanged.
We instead analyse distilling the feature.
Minimizing this loss keeps the attacked feature close to the teacher's clean feature, and the same
term limits how far the feature moves inside the perturbation ball.
Distilling the feature improves clean accuracy and robustness together.

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $f=h\circ\Phi$ be a classifier with feature map $\Phi$ and linear head $h$. Adversarial training
\citep{PGD} solves
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\ \max_{x'\in B(x,\epsilon)}\ \ell\big(f_\theta(x'),y\big),
\qquad B(x,\epsilon)=\{x':\lVert x'-x\rVert_\infty\le\epsilon\}.
\label{eqn:at}
\end{equation}
The supervision in \Cref{eqn:at} is the label $y$.  Its inner problem searches the ball for an input
at which the classifier disagrees most strongly with that label, whereas natural training constrains
the prediction only at $x$.
We write $f_t = h_t \circ \Phi_t$ for a network of the same architecture trained naturally on the same
data, frozen and used as the teacher throughout, with feature $\Phi_t(x)$ and logits $f_t(x)$.

\subsection{Where a Natural Teacher Fails, and Where It Does Not}
\label{sec:fragile}

Existing methods commonly use the logits of a naturally trained network, either alongside the label
loss or together with another teacher \citep{lbgat, arrest, bmtard, dpfat}.  This leaves two choices:
which teacher quantity to match, and where to evaluate the teacher.  We examine them separately.

\paragraph{Logit matching benefits from softening, but does not recover clean accuracy.}
Much of the distillation literature treats target sharpness as the main difficulty.  LTD
\citep{LTD}, RSLAD \citep{rslad}, ADR \citep{adr}, and B-MTARD \citep{bmtard} consequently modify the
temperature or entropy of a logit target.  In this family of methods, the transferred object is
$\mathrm{softmax}(z_t / \tau)$ and $\tau$ determines its sharpness.

Not every method poses the question in this form, and \Cref{sec:related} places the exceptions.
LBGAT \citep{lbgat} in particular matches logits under a squared error with no softmax at all, at the
cost of training its natural branch jointly.

The natural teacher is an extreme instance of this setting.  Its mean maximum softmax probability on
clean test data is $0.820$, compared with $0.016$ for the adversarially trained student in this
comparison.  Direct logit matching therefore supplies a substantially sharper target than the
student's own output.

In a fixed base regime---50 epochs, no weight averaging, no AWP, and $\epsilon = 8/255$---increasing
$\tau$ improves AutoAttack accuracy from $20.84$ at $\tau = 1$ to $24.48$ at $\tau = 4$; it then
declines at $\tau = 16$.  Thus, target sharpness matters in this comparison.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
  \toprule
  target & Clean & AA & NRR \\
  \midrule
  teacher logits, $\tau = 1$  & 58.26 & 20.84 & 30.70 \\
  teacher logits, $\tau = 4$  & 59.39 & 24.48 & 34.67 \\
  teacher logits, $\tau = 16$ & 57.78 & 24.00 & 33.91 \\
  \midrule
  \textbf{teacher feature}    & \textbf{62.72} & \textbf{25.88} & \textbf{36.64} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The temperature sweep does not similarly improve clean accuracy: it remains between $57.78$ and
$59.39$, while the teacher reaches $77.66$.  Under the same regime and at the same input, matching
the teacher's feature instead gives a further $1.40$ points of AutoAttack accuracy and $3.33$ points
of clean accuracy, without a temperature.

These results distinguish two effects.  Softening improves the robustness of logit matching in this
setting, but it does not close the clean-accuracy gap.  The feature target performs better on both
metrics, suggesting that useful information from the natural teacher is not adequately represented by
its softened softmax output.

\paragraph{A natural teacher is not a stable target inside the ball.} Under
$\epsilon = 8/255$, the teacher's feature rotates by $63.8^\circ$ and its norm grows by a factor of
$2.45$.  Its representation inside the ball is therefore quite different from its representation at
the clean input.  This matters particularly for methods that evaluate the teacher at perturbed
points. IGDM \citep{igdm} matches a finite difference
$f_t(x{+}\delta) - f_t(x{-}\delta)$ as an approximation to the teacher's input gradient. This
approximation is informative only when the teacher is locally close to linear. IGDM reports a Taylor
remainder proportion of $0.012$ for adversarially trained teachers. We obtain $0.0111$ for an
adversarially trained ResNet-18 and $\mathbf{0.4763}$ for the natural teacher. For the latter, the
finite difference has norm $14.14$, compared with $\lVert f_t(x) \rVert = 18.36$, and its softmax puts
$0.917$ of its mass on one class. These measurements indicate that this finite-difference target is
poorly behaved for the natural teacher.

\paragraph{Published logit objectives with a natural teacher.} \Cref{tab:natteacher} evaluates
published objectives after replacing their robust teacher with our natural teacher, using each
method's published recipe. All of them are below adversarial training initialized from that teacher.
In this setting, the logit-based route does not transfer the teacher's clean accuracy effectively.

These observations motivate the construction below: the teacher is evaluated at $x$, and the target
is its feature rather than its logits.

\subsection{What a Clean-Feature Anchor Controls}
\label{sec:decomp}

Consider matching the teacher's feature at the \emph{clean} input against the student's feature at the
\emph{adversarial} input, that is, minimizing
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(x) \;=\; \max_{x' \in B(x,\epsilon)} \big\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert^2 .
\label{eqn:anchor_percell}
\end{equation}
Equation \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} asks the student's feature at every attacked input to approach one
fixed teacher feature. It therefore combines agreement with the teacher at the clean input and limited
variation of the student's feature within the ball. The following observation makes that relation
explicit.

Fix $x$, write $B = B(x,\epsilon)$, and define
\begin{align}
L &= \max_{x' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:L}\\
F &= \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:F}\\
O &= \max_{x', x'' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert . \label{eqn:O}
\end{align}
Here $L$ is the square root of \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell}; $F$ measures clean feature agreement with
the teacher; and $O$ is the diameter of the student's feature map over the ball. The latter is a
representation-level notion of local variation, not a direct measure of classification robustness.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decomp}
For every $x$ and every $\epsilon \ge 0$,
\begin{equation}
L \;\le\; F + O \;\le\; 3L .
\label{eqn:decomp}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the right-hand inequality, $F \le L$ because $x \in B$, so the maximum in \Cref{eqn:L} is at least
the value at $x$. For $O$, insert $\Phi_t(x)$ between the two student features: for any
$x', x'' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_t(x) - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; 2L ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x', x''$ gives $O \le 2L$. Adding the two bounds gives $F + O \le 3L$.
For the left-hand inequality, insert $\Phi_s(x)$ instead: for any $x' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; O + F ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x'$ gives $L \le F + O$.
\end{proof}

The result uses only the triangle inequality. It therefore requires no smoothness or linearity
assumption, and it holds independently of the architecture or of how the maximum is approximated.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:both}
If the anchor is driven to $L \le \delta$ at $x$, then $F \le \delta$ and $O \le 2\delta$ at $x$.
\end{corollary}

Thus, reducing the anchor bounds both quantities. Conversely, the left inequality shows that the
anchor cannot be small unless both fidelity error and feature variation are small. This differs from
using a weighted sum of separate terms, where their relative scale is set by a coefficient and must
be chosen externally. The anchor has no such coefficient because both properties arise from the same
distance to a fixed target.

\paragraph{The constant.} The factor $3$ is $1+2$: $F \le L$, and $O \le 2L$. The latter bound is
tight when two student features lie on opposite sides of $\Phi_t(x)$, each at distance $L$.

\paragraph{Why the teacher is read only at the clean input.}
Equation \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} evaluates $\Phi_t$ only at $x$, both in the loss and in the inner
maximization. The teacher's behavior elsewhere in $B$---including the instability measured in
\Cref{sec:fragile}---is consequently not used as a target. This does not make the teacher irrelevant:
its clean feature geometry and classifier define the representation the student is asked to preserve.
It does, however, separate that clean guidance from the teacher's adversarial behavior.

The claim is testable by moving the input at which the teacher is evaluated and changing nothing
else. Replacing $\Phi_t(x)$ with $\Phi_t(x')$ in both the loss and the attack drives AutoAttack accuracy
from $26.19$ to $0.00$ while clean accuracy \emph{rises} to $76.11$, within $1.55$ of the natural
teacher (\Cref{tab:controls}). The failure is not that a moving target is harder to track, but that
$\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x') \rVert$ is minimized exactly by $\Phi_s = \Phi_t$, so a student that
copies the natural teacher everywhere is optimal and has no robustness at all. Evaluating the teacher
at the clean input therefore prevents this degenerate natural-copy solution.

\paragraph{Empirical check.} On CIFAR-100 over $\epsilon=8/255$ balls, we measure $L=7.58$,
$F=6.30$, and a lower bound $O_{\mathrm{lb}}=2.16$, compared with $O=6.68$ for the teacher on the
same balls. The student is therefore more stable in this feature-space measurement, despite using a
natural teacher. Moreover, $F \gg O_{\mathrm{lb}}$, indicating that the remaining anchor loss is not
explained solely by a flat student representation.

\paragraph{Scope of the proposition.} \Cref{prop:decomp} is a statement about the objective and the
student representation. It does not lower-bound robust accuracy or establish a classification margin.
Those require properties of the classifier and of the class geometry that the inequality does not
assume. The robustness gains reported below are therefore empirical results, not consequences of the
proposition.

The empirical result is stronger than this representation-level observation, and it appears to arise
for a different reason. Asked to work with neither weight averaging nor AWP nor a
sensitivity-matched radius---the anchor alone, against label cross-entropy under an identical
schedule, initialization and training radius---the anchor leads by $6.69$ clean \emph{and} $5.91$
AutoAttack, and by $4.63$ and $4.45$ against cross-entropy run at its own optimizer and schedule
instead of ours (\Cref{tab:controls}, \Cref{tab:recipe}). The two students have similar relative
feature displacement under a matched attack (\Cref{sec:components}), so this comparison does not
support local feature variation as the sole mechanism. Their class separation differs by a factor of
$5.5$, which is the more informative correlate in our measurements. At the full stack, CFA also
leads cross-entropy with weight averaging and AWP by $2.40$ AutoAttack points. We therefore treat the
robustness gain as an empirical effect associated with the learned class geometry; the proposition
explains a property of the objective, not the magnitude of that gain.
