\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $f=h\circ\Phi$ be a classifier with feature map $\Phi$ and linear head $h$. Adversarial training
\citep{PGD} solves
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\ \max_{x'\in B(x,\epsilon)}\ \ell\big(f_\theta(x'),y\big),
\qquad B(x,\epsilon)=\{x':\lVert x'-x\rVert_\infty\le\epsilon\}.
\label{eqn:at}
\end{equation}
The supervisory signal is the label, and it is asserted uniformly over $B(x,\epsilon)$: every point
of the ball must reach full confidence in one class. That is where the accuracy cost comes from.
We write $f_t = h_t \circ \Phi_t$ for a network of the same architecture trained naturally on the same
data---the teacher throughout, frozen---so that $\Phi_t(x)$ is its feature and $f_t(x)$ its logits, and
ask what it can supply.

\subsection{Where a Natural Teacher Fails, and Where It Does Not}
\label{sec:fragile}

A method that consumes a teacher can go wrong in two independent places: \emph{where} it reads the
teacher, and \emph{what} it reads from it. Both defects are present when a naturally trained network is
used the way adversarial distillation uses one, and they can be measured separately.

\paragraph{What is read: the field's prescription is softening, and it is only half a fix.}
Four separate lines of work converge on the same diagnosis---the target is too sharp---and each builds
its own machinery to soften it. LTD \citep{LTD} argues that one-hot labels are the wrong supervision
for adversarial training and replaces them with soft ones. RSLAD \citep{rslad} makes the smoothness of
the teacher's logits its central claim. ADR \citep{adr} draws its target from a weight average and
scales it by a softmax temperature that starts near-uniform and is annealed down over training.
B-MTARD \citep{bmtard} formalizes the quantity being controlled---writing the teacher's knowledge
scale as $\log C - H(P_T)$, so that a sharper teacher carries a larger one---and adjusts each teacher's
temperature online to equalize it. In each of these the operative object is
$\mathrm{softmax}(z_t / \tau)$ and the design question is $\tau$.

Not every method that consumes a natural network poses it, and \Cref{sec:related} places the
exceptions; LBGAT \citep{lbgat} in particular matches logits under a squared error with no softmax at
all, at the cost of training its natural branch jointly.

By that account a naturally trained teacher is the extreme case, and the measurement agrees: on clean
test data its maximum softmax probability is $0.820$, against $0.016$ for the adversarially trained
student it is supposed to teach. It is asking for a confidence twenty times sharper than the student's
own.

The prescription follows, and it works. Sweeping $\tau$ on that teacher's logits in a fixed base
regime---50 epochs, no weight averaging, no AWP, $\epsilon = 8/255$---AutoAttack accuracy rises from
$20.84$ at $\tau = 1$ to $24.48$ at $\tau = 4$, then turns over. Sharpness is a genuine defect and
softening genuinely repairs it.

What it does not repair is the accuracy. Across the whole sweep clean accuracy moves between $57.78$
and $59.39$---it is flat in $\tau$---while the teacher it is being distilled from has $77.66$. Reading
the same teacher's \emph{feature} instead, with no temperature anywhere, gains a further $1.40$
AutoAttack and $3.33$ clean, at the same read point and under the same regime.

The two numbers separate cleanly. \textbf{Temperature moves robustness and leaves accuracy where it
was; the feature moves both.} Sharpness is therefore a real problem with a real fix, and it is not the
problem this paper is trying to solve: the accuracy a naturally trained network holds does not survive
its own softmax, and no choice of $\tau$ puts it back. It survives one layer earlier.

\paragraph{Where it is read: off the clean point the teacher has nothing to say.} Under
$\epsilon = 8/255$ the teacher's own feature rotates by $63.8^\circ$ and its norm inflates by
$\times 2.45$, so its output inside the ball is not a softened version of its output at the centre. The
consequence is sharpest for methods that read it there. IGDM \citep{igdm} matches a finite difference
$f_t(x{+}\delta) - f_t(x{-}\delta)$ as a stand-in for the teacher's input gradient, which is a gradient
only while the teacher is locally linear; its own diagnostic reports a Taylor remainder proportion of
$0.012$ for adversarially trained teachers. Applying that diagnostic to our checkpoints gives $0.0111$
for an adversarially trained ResNet-18---reproducing their value, so the measurement is
calibrated---and $\mathbf{0.4763}$ for the natural teacher, forty times further from linear. The target
it constructs is correspondingly degenerate: $\lVert f_t(x{+}\delta) - f_t(x{-}\delta) \rVert = 14.14$
against $\lVert f_t(x) \rVert = 18.36$, with the softmax of that difference placing $0.917$ of its mass
on one class.

\paragraph{Together, they cost more than the teacher is worth.} \Cref{tab:natteacher} gives the
published objectives with our natural teacher in place of the robust one they assume, each at its own
published recipe. Every one lands below plain adversarial training initialized from that same
teacher---that is, below not distilling at all. The accuracy the teacher holds is real, and the logit
route does not recover it.

What follows keeps the read point at $x$, where the second defect cannot arise, and takes the features,
which the first measurement shows to be the better read.

\subsection{What a Clean-Feature Anchor Controls}
\label{sec:decomp}

Consider matching the teacher's feature at the \emph{clean} input against the student's feature at the
\emph{adversarial} input, that is, minimizing
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(x) \;=\; \max_{x' \in B(x,\epsilon)} \big\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert^2 .
\label{eqn:anchor_percell}
\end{equation}
Read literally, \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} asks the student to reproduce a \emph{fixed} vector from
every point of the ball. That is a fidelity constraint, and a fidelity constraint alone would have no
reason to produce robustness. It turns out not to be one, and the reason is visible once the quantity
is compared with the two properties one would otherwise have to impose separately.

Fix $x$, write $B = B(x,\epsilon)$, and define
\begin{align}
L &= \max_{x' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:L}\\
F &= \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:F}\\
O &= \max_{x', x'' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert . \label{eqn:O}
\end{align}
Here $L$ is the square root of \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell}, the quantity the method minimizes; $F$ is
\emph{fidelity}, how far the student is from the teacher at the clean point; and $O$ is
\emph{oscillation}, the diameter of the student's own feature map over the ball. $F$ is what
adversarial training gives up relative to natural training, and $O$ is what its label term buys. They
are ordinarily two objectives.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decomp}
For every $x$ and every $\epsilon \ge 0$,
\begin{equation}
L \;\le\; F + O \;\le\; 3L .
\label{eqn:decomp}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the right-hand inequality, $F \le L$ because $x \in B$, so the maximum in \Cref{eqn:L} is at least
the value at $x$. For $O$, insert $\Phi_t(x)$ between the two student features: for any
$x', x'' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_t(x) - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; 2L ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x', x''$ gives $O \le 2L$. Adding the two bounds gives $F + O \le 3L$.
For the left-hand inequality, insert $\Phi_s(x)$ instead: for any $x' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; O + F ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x'$ gives $L \le F + O$.
\end{proof}

Only the triangle inequality is used, so \Cref{prop:decomp} holds for the networks we actually train:
no smoothness, no linearity, no assumption on the architecture or on the attack that realizes the
maximum. Two consequences follow immediately.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:both}
If the anchor is driven to $L \le \delta$ at $x$, then $F \le \delta$ and $O \le 2\delta$ at $x$.
\end{corollary}

That is the operative statement: driving one scalar down bounds \emph{both} properties, at rates that
differ by a factor of two and by nothing else. Conversely, by the left inequality, $L$ cannot be small
unless both are, so the objective admits no solution that satisfies one at the expense of the other.
A weighted sum $F + \lambda O$ has no such property for any fixed $\lambda$: its level sets contain
points where either term is arbitrarily large. This is why the anchor is stated as the whole objective
rather than as a regularizer, and why there is no coefficient to select.

\paragraph{Where the constant $3$ comes from, and how tight it is.} The factor is $1 + 2$: one from
$F \le L$ and two from $O \le 2L$, and the second is attained when the student's outputs at the two
extreme points of the ball sit on opposite sides of $\Phi_t(x)$ at distance $L$ each. Our trained
students are far from that configuration, which is the content of the measurement below.

\paragraph{The teacher appears at $x$ and nowhere else.} \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} evaluates $\Phi_t$
at the clean point only, in the loss and in the attack that realizes the maximum. Whatever the teacher
does inside $B$ is therefore absent from the objective, and the instability measured in
\Cref{sec:fragile}---$63.8^\circ$ of rotation, $\times 2.45$ of norm---has no path into the
optimization. The same fact settles the symmetric question: since the teacher is never asked to resist
a perturbation, it cannot supply robustness either, and all of it is produced by the student's inner
maximization.

This is testable by moving the read point and changing nothing else, and the test is decisive.
Replacing $\Phi_t(x)$ with $\Phi_t(x')$ in both the loss and the attack drives AutoAttack accuracy
from $26.19$ to $0.00$ while clean accuracy \emph{rises} to $76.11$, within $1.55$ of the natural
teacher (\Cref{tab:controls}). The failure is not that a moving target is harder to track. It is that
$\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x') \rVert$ is minimized exactly by $\Phi_s = \Phi_t$, so the student
that copies a natural teacher everywhere is optimal---and it has no robustness at all. The clean read
point is what makes \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} a non-degenerate objective, and the same asymmetry that
keeps the teacher's fragility out is what keeps the trivial solution out.

\paragraph{Verification.} \Cref{prop:decomp} predicts that a student trained on $L$ ends up with a
small $O$, and the prediction is checkable on the trained network. Measured over $\epsilon = 8/255$
balls on CIFAR-100, $L = 7.58$, $F = 6.30$ and $O_{\mathrm{lb}} = 2.16$, against the teacher's own
$O = 6.68$: the student is $3.1\times$ more stable than the network it was distilled from, on the same
balls. Two things follow. The teacher's instability was not inherited, which is what the construction
predicts. And $F \gg O_{\mathrm{lb}}$, so what remains in the loss at convergence is fidelity rather
than instability---the anchor is not being satisfied by making the student flat.

\paragraph{What the proposition is for, and what it is not.} \Cref{prop:decomp} says the anchor does
not \emph{cost} robustness: local stability is bounded by the same scalar as fidelity, so the two
cannot be traded against each other. It is not a lower bound on how much robustness the objective
buys, and we do not read it as one; \Cref{app:robustness} gives two partial results in that direction,
each with the reason it falls short.

The measurement is stronger than the proposition, and it is stronger for a different reason than the
proposition would suggest. Asked to work with neither weight averaging nor AWP nor a
sensitivity-matched radius---the anchor alone, against label cross-entropy under an identical
schedule, initialization and training radius---the anchor leads by $6.61$ clean \emph{and} $5.79$
AutoAttack (\Cref{tab:controls}). The obvious reading is that \Cref{prop:decomp}'s bound on $O$ is
doing the work, and \Cref{sec:components} shows it is not: the two students oscillate by the same
relative amount under a matched attack. What differs is class separation, by a factor of $5.5$, and
that is where we locate the effect. For scale, the full recipe leads cross-entropy \emph{with} weight
averaging and AWP by $2.40$. So the anchor is a source of robustness rather than a neutral passenger
on one, and at matched stack it is the larger source; the components in \Cref{tab:ladder} are
additive on top of it rather than the origin of the effect. We keep \Cref{prop:decomp} stated as the
weaker claim because that is what the inequality supports---the $5.79$ is measured, not derived.
