\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

We examine how a naturally trained teacher can help preserve clean accuracy during adversarial
training. Prior work motivates the use of soft targets in place of one-hot labels
\citep{ard, rslad, LTD, adr}, but it remains unclear whether softening the predictions of a natural
teacher is sufficient to recover its clean accuracy. We therefore examine feature distillation as
an alternative, focusing on the choice of teacher target. Our analysis shows how distilling the
teacher's clean features into the student's adversarial features can jointly control fidelity to the
teacher and stability under input perturbations through a single objective.

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $f=h\circ\Phi$ be a classifier with feature map $\Phi$ and linear head $h$. Adversarial training
\citep{PGD} solves
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\ \max_{x'\in B(x,\epsilon)}\ \ell\big(f_\theta(x'),y\big),
\qquad B(x,\epsilon)=\{x':\lVert x'-x\rVert_\infty\le\epsilon\}.
\label{eqn:at}
\end{equation}
The inner maximization finds a perturbation that maximizes the supervised loss, and the outer
minimization updates the classifier on that perturbed input.
We write $f_t = h_t \circ \Phi_t$ for a network of the same architecture trained naturally on the same
data, frozen and used as the teacher throughout, with feature $\Phi_t(x)$ and logits $f_t(x)$.

\subsection{Choosing the Distillation Target}
\label{sec:fragile}

Using a natural teacher requires specifying both the supervisory target and the input supplied to
the teacher. We first compare softened predictions with features, then examine the effect of
evaluating the teacher on perturbed inputs.

\paragraph{Softened predictions and feature targets.}
Target sharpness is a consideration in adversarial distillation \citep{LTD, rslad, adr, bmtard}.
For a target of the form $\mathrm{softmax}(f_t(x)/\tau)$, increasing the temperature $\tau$ reduces
the concentration of the predicted distribution. Our natural teacher has a maximum softmax
probability of $0.820$ on clean test data, compared with $0.016$ for the adversarially trained
student in this comparison. We therefore examine whether softening this target is sufficient to
improve the student.

In a fixed base regime---50 epochs, no weight averaging, no AWP, and $\epsilon = 8/255$---increasing
$\tau$ improves AutoAttack accuracy from $20.84$ at $\tau = 1$ to $24.48$ at $\tau = 4$; it then
declines at $\tau = 16$.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
  \toprule
  target & Clean & AA & NRR \\
  \midrule
  teacher logits, $\tau = 1$  & 58.26 & 20.84 & 30.70 \\
  teacher logits, $\tau = 4$  & 59.39 & 24.48 & 34.67 \\
  teacher logits, $\tau = 16$ & 57.78 & 24.00 & 33.91 \\
  \midrule
  \textbf{teacher feature}    & \textbf{62.72} & \textbf{25.88} & \textbf{36.64} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The temperature sweep does not similarly improve clean accuracy: it remains between $57.78$ and
$59.39$, while the teacher reaches $77.66$.  Under the same regime and at the same input, matching
the teacher's feature instead gives a further $1.40$ points of AutoAttack accuracy and $3.33$ points
of clean accuracy, without a temperature.

The improvement from softening is therefore insufficient to explain the advantage of feature
matching in this experiment. Direct feature supervision provides a more effective target under the
tested conditions. This comparison concerns softened predictions; methods that match logits without
a softmax, such as LBGAT \citep{lbgat}, use a different objective.

\paragraph{Teacher evaluation on perturbed inputs.} Under
$\epsilon = 8/255$, the teacher's feature rotates by $63.8^\circ$ and its norm grows by a factor of
$2.45$.  Its representation inside the ball is therefore quite different from its representation at
the clean input.  This matters particularly for methods that evaluate the teacher at perturbed
points. IGDM \citep{igdm} matches a finite difference
$f_t(x{+}\delta) - f_t(x{-}\delta)$ to approximate the teacher's response along the perturbation
direction. The accuracy of this approximation depends on the local linearity of the teacher. IGDM reports a Taylor
remainder proportion of $0.012$ for adversarially trained teachers. We obtain $0.0111$ for an
adversarially trained ResNet-18 and $\mathbf{0.4763}$ for the natural teacher. For the latter, the
finite difference has norm $14.14$, compared with $\lVert f_t(x) \rVert = 18.36$, and its softmax puts
$0.917$ of its mass on one class. The larger remainder indicates a less accurate local linear
approximation for the natural teacher, while the resulting target distribution is highly concentrated.

\paragraph{Published logit objectives with a natural teacher.} \Cref{tab:natteacher} evaluates
published objectives after replacing their robust teacher with our natural teacher, using each
method's published recipe. All of them are below adversarial training initialized from that teacher.
In this setting, the logit-based route does not transfer the teacher's clean accuracy effectively.

Together, these observations motivate using the teacher's clean feature as a fixed target. The next
subsection examines how this choice allows teacher fidelity and local feature stability to be
controlled by a single distillation objective.

\subsection{What a Clean-Feature Anchor Controls}
\label{sec:decomp}

Consider matching the teacher's feature at the \emph{clean} input against the student's feature at the
\emph{adversarial} input, that is, minimizing
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(x) \;=\; \max_{x' \in B(x,\epsilon)} \big\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert^2 .
\label{eqn:anchor_percell}
\end{equation}
The target $\Phi_t(x)$ is fixed throughout the inner maximization. Consequently, a small value of
\Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} requires the student's features throughout the ball, including at the clean
input, to remain close to the same teacher feature. We formalize this connection below.

Fix $x$, write $B = B(x,\epsilon)$, and define
\begin{align}
L &= \max_{x' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:L}\\
F &= \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:F}\\
O &= \max_{x', x'' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert . \label{eqn:O}
\end{align}
Here $L$ is the square root of \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell}; $F$ measures clean feature agreement with
the teacher; and $O$ is the diameter of the student's feature map over the ball. The latter is a
representation-level notion of local variation, not a direct measure of classification robustness.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decomp}
For every $x$ and every $\epsilon \ge 0$,
\begin{equation}
L \;\le\; F + O \;\le\; 3L .
\label{eqn:decomp}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the right-hand inequality, $F \le L$ because $x \in B$, so the maximum in \Cref{eqn:L} is at least
the value at $x$. For $O$, insert $\Phi_t(x)$ between the two student features: for any
$x', x'' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_t(x) - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; 2L ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x', x''$ gives $O \le 2L$. Adding the two bounds gives $F + O \le 3L$.
For the left-hand inequality, insert $\Phi_s(x)$ instead: for any $x' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; O + F ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x'$ gives $L \le F + O$.
\end{proof}

The result follows from the triangle inequality and requires no smoothness or linearity assumption.
The bounds concern the exact maxima in the definitions of $L$ and $O$; evaluating the loss using a
finite attack does not certify these maxima.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:both}
If the anchor is driven to $L \le \delta$ at $x$, then $F \le \delta$ and $O \le 2\delta$ at $x$.
\end{corollary}

The two inequalities relate the anchor to the sum of clean feature discrepancy and local feature
variation up to a constant factor. A small anchor bounds both quantities, and small values of both
also imply a small anchor. This relation motivates using the anchor as the sole backbone objective,
without introducing a coefficient between separate fidelity and stability terms. The factor $3$
combines $F\le L$ with $O\le 2L$; the latter bound is attained when two student features lie on
opposite sides of the target, each at distance $L$.

The same argument applies to any fixed target in a normed space, including a logit target. Its role
here is to explain the joint control provided by the anchor. The choice of features is motivated by
the comparison in \Cref{sec:fragile} and allows the student to retain the teacher's classifier, as
described in \Cref{sec:anchor}.

\paragraph{The role of the fixed clean target.}
In \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell}, the teacher supplies $\Phi_t(x)$ while the perturbation is optimized
through the student. The teacher contributes its clean representation, without requiring its
responses to perturbed inputs to serve as supervision. Its clean feature geometry remains relevant
to the student's predictions through the inherited classifier.

If the target is instead $\Phi_t(x')$, the student can achieve zero loss by setting
$\Phi_s=\Phi_t$, regardless of the teacher's sensitivity to perturbations. This objective therefore
admits an exact copy of the natural teacher as a global minimizer. In the corresponding control,
replacing $\Phi_t(x)$ with $\Phi_t(x')$ in both the loss and attack reduces AutoAttack accuracy from
$26.19$ to $0.00$, while clean accuracy increases to $76.11$, within $1.55$ points of the teacher
(\Cref{tab:controls}). With a fixed clean target, copying the teacher incurs a nonzero loss wherever
its features change within the ball.

\paragraph{Empirical check.} On CIFAR-100 over $\epsilon=8/255$ balls, we measure $L=7.58$,
$F=6.30$, and a lower bound $O_{\mathrm{lb}}=2.16$, compared with $O=6.68$ for the teacher on the
same balls. The observed student variation is smaller in this measurement, and a substantial clean
feature discrepancy remains. Since $O_{\mathrm{lb}}$ is a lower bound, these measurements do not
establish the student's worst-case variation or determine which quantity dominates the exact bound.

\paragraph{Scope of the proposition.} \Cref{prop:decomp} is a statement about the objective and the
student representation. It does not lower-bound robust accuracy or establish a classification margin.
Those require properties of the classifier and of the class geometry that the inequality does not
assume. Nor does joint control imply that both errors can be made arbitrarily small across the
dataset: overlapping perturbation balls with different teacher targets impose conflicting
constraints on the student. The proposition characterizes how the objective controls the two
quantities, without eliminating this possible conflict.

The classification benefit is assessed empirically in \Cref{sec:components}. Without weight
averaging, AWP, or the sensitivity-matched radius, the anchor improves clean and AutoAttack accuracy
over label cross-entropy by $6.69$ and $5.91$ points under the same training conditions, and by
$4.63$ and $4.45$ points when cross-entropy uses its own optimizer and schedule
(\Cref{tab:controls}, \Cref{tab:recipe}). At the full training configuration, the AutoAttack gain
over cross-entropy with weight averaging and AWP is $2.40$ points. Under a matched attack, the two
students exhibit similar relative feature displacement, whereas their class separation differs by a
factor of $5.5$. These observations suggest that the geometry preserved by the anchor contributes to
its effectiveness; they do not establish class separation as the sole cause of the robustness gain.
