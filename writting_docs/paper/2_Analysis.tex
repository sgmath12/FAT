\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

We examine how a naturally trained teacher can help preserve clean accuracy during adversarial
training. Prior work motivates the use of soft targets in place of one-hot labels
\citep{ard, rslad, LTD, adr}, but it remains unclear whether softening the predictions of a natural
teacher is sufficient to recover its clean accuracy. We therefore examine feature distillation as
an alternative, focusing on the choice of teacher target. Our analysis shows how distilling the
teacher's clean features into the student's adversarial features can jointly control fidelity to the
teacher and stability under input perturbations through a single objective.

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $f=h\circ\Phi$ be a classifier with feature map $\Phi$ and linear head $h$. Adversarial training
\citep{PGD} solves
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\ \max_{x'\in B(x,\epsilon)}\ \ell\big(f_\theta(x'),y\big),
\qquad B(x,\epsilon)=\{x':\lVert x'-x\rVert_\infty\le\epsilon\}.
\label{eqn:at}
\end{equation}
The inner maximization finds a perturbation that maximizes the supervised loss, and the outer
minimization updates the classifier on that perturbed input.
We write $f_t = h_t \circ \Phi_t$ for a network of the same architecture trained naturally on the same
data, frozen and used as the teacher throughout, with feature $\Phi_t(x)$ and logits $f_t(x)$.

\subsection{Choosing the Distillation Target}
\label{sec:fragile}

A naturally trained teacher provides accurate but highly confident predictions, raising the question
of whether target softening can make them suitable for adversarial training. Prior work highlights
the importance of soft supervisory distributions for robustness \citep{rslad, adr} and uses predictive
entropy to adjust guidance from natural and robust teachers \citep{bmtard}. Increasing temperature
produces less concentrated predictions, with higher entropy and lower maximum class probability.
We use these two statistics to quantify target softness throughout the comparison.

We test this by distilling targets $q_t^\tau(x)=\mathrm{softmax}(f_t(x)/\tau)$ from a fixed natural
teacher using $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_t^\tau(x)\,\|\,\mathrm{softmax}(f_s(x_{\mathrm{adv}})))$, where
$\tau$ is applied only to the teacher target, leaving the student's softmax temperature at one
throughout the sweep, unlike conventional KD with a shared temperature for both distributions
\citep{hinton2015distilling}.
The same KL loss is maximized over $B(x,\epsilon)$ to generate adversarial examples and minimized
to train the student, without an additional ground-truth label loss.

For comparison, we distill the teacher's features by minimizing
$\lVert\Phi_s(x_{\mathrm{adv}})-\Phi_t(x)\rVert_2^2$, with adversarial examples generated by
maximizing the same distance and the classifier $h_t$ kept fixed. We evaluate both forms of
supervision on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18, reporting student performance alongside the maximum
probability and entropy of $q_t^\tau(x)$ averaged over clean test inputs.

As shown in \Cref{tab:targetcomparison}, increasing temperature raises target entropy and reduces
the maximum class probability, accompanied by improved AutoAttack accuracy up to $\tau=8$.
Further softening brings the targets closer to a uniform distribution but reduces robustness.
Clean accuracy, however, changes little across the sweep and remains well below the teacher's
$77.66\%$, indicating that target softening alone is insufficient to recover its clean performance.
On the other hand, feature matching achieves higher clean and AutoAttack accuracy than all logit
targets tested. This result motivates examining how feature supervision can preserve agreement
with the teacher while encouraging stability under perturbations.

\subsection{What a Clean-Feature Anchor Controls}
\label{sec:decomp}

We now analyze the feature matching objective introduced in \Cref{sec:fragile}. Our aim is to
establish how a single target can constrain both the student's discrepancy from the teacher and
its response to perturbations. For each input $x$, the clean-feature anchor is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(x) \;=\; \max_{x' \in B(x,\epsilon)} \big\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert^2 .
\label{eqn:anchor_percell}
\end{equation}
The teacher feature $\Phi_t(x)$ is fixed as the student input varies within the ball. Bringing all
of these student features close to the same target also brings them close to one another.
\Cref{prop:decomp} formalizes this intuition by relating the anchor to two quantities: the student's
discrepancy from the teacher at the clean input and the variation of its own features across the ball.

Fix $x$, write $B = B(x,\epsilon)$, and define
\begin{align}
L &= \max_{x' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:L}\\
F &= \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:F}\\
O &= \max_{x', x'' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert . \label{eqn:O}
\end{align}
Here $L$ is the square root of \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell}. The fidelity error $F$ measures how closely
the student matches the teacher at the clean input. The oscillation $O$ measures the largest
distance between any two student features in the ball, and thus describes local stability in feature
space.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decomp}
For every $x$ and every $\epsilon \ge 0$,
\begin{equation}
L \;\le\; F + O \;\le\; 3L .
\label{eqn:decomp}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the right-hand inequality, $F \le L$ because $x \in B$, so the maximum in \Cref{eqn:L} is at least
the value at $x$. For $O$, insert $\Phi_t(x)$ between the two student features: for any
$x', x'' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_t(x) - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; 2L ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x', x''$ gives $O \le 2L$. Adding the two bounds gives $F + O \le 3L$.
For the left-hand inequality, insert $\Phi_s(x)$ instead: for any $x' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; O + F ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x'$ gives $L \le F + O$.
\end{proof}

The result uses only the triangle inequality. Its direct consequence is that a small anchor
simultaneously bounds both the clean fidelity error and local feature variation.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:both}
If the anchor is driven to $L \le \delta$ at $x$, then $F \le \delta$ and $O \le 2\delta$ at $x$.
\end{corollary}

The inequalities show that the anchor and $F+O$ are equivalent up to a constant factor. Thus,
keeping every adversarial feature close to the teacher's clean feature also limits the student's
clean discrepancy and its variation under perturbations. This gives a rationale for using the
anchor as the sole backbone objective: fidelity and local feature stability are controlled through
the same distance, without a coefficient between separate loss terms.
The relation characterizes the objective in feature space; its implications and limitations are
discussed in \Cref{app:anchor_scope}.

\paragraph{Empirical check.}
Under the evaluated perturbations, the trained student exhibits less feature variation than the
natural teacher, while retaining a nonzero clean discrepancy from it
(\Cref{app:anchor_measurements}). This indicates that the student can learn a less sensitive
representation from a natural teacher without reproducing its response to those perturbations.
These observations are consistent with the role of the fixed anchor in the analysis.
The resulting classification performance and the geometry of the learned representation are
examined in \Cref{sec:components}.
