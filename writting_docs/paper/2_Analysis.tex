\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $f=h\circ\Phi$ be a classifier with feature map $\Phi$ and linear head $h$. Adversarial training
\citep{PGD} solves
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\ \max_{x'\in B(x,\epsilon)}\ \ell\big(f_\theta(x'),y\big),
\qquad B(x,\epsilon)=\{x':\lVert x'-x\rVert_\infty\le\epsilon\}.
\label{eqn:at}
\end{equation}
The supervisory signal is the label, and it is asserted uniformly over $B(x,\epsilon)$: every point
of the ball must reach full confidence in one class. That is where the accuracy cost comes from.
We write $\Phi_t$ for the frozen feature map of a network of the same architecture trained naturally on
the same data---the teacher throughout---and ask what it can supply.

\subsection{The Teacher Is Fragile, and Its Logits Are Worse Than No Teacher at All}
\label{sec:fragile}

The objection to a naturally trained teacher is not hypothetical. Under $\epsilon=8/255$ its own
feature rotates by $63.8^\circ$ and its norm inflates by $\times2.45$; its accuracy rests on
directions an adversary controls.

That fragility appears immediately if the teacher is consumed the way adversarial distillation
consumes one. \Cref{tab:natteacher} gives the published objectives with our natural teacher in
place of the robust teacher they assume, each at its own published recipe, against plain adversarial
training initialized from the same teacher.

None of it is a temperature that was left untuned: swept in a fixed base regime, the logit target
peaks at $24.48$ AutoAttack ($\tau=4$) and the curve is unimodal because $\tau$ is squeezed from both
sides---at $\tau=1$ the teacher's maximum probability is $0.820$ against the student's $0.016$, and at
$\tau=16$ the target is within $1\%$ of uniform.

\paragraph{Two defects, and they separate.} A method can go wrong in \emph{where} it reads the teacher
and in \emph{what} it reads from it, and \Cref{tab:natteacher} confounds them. Holding the read
point fixed at $x$ and changing only what is read---the teacher's logits against its features, same
regime, same attack, same teacher---gives $24.48\to\mathbf{25.88}$ AutoAttack. That isolates the
second defect.

The first is larger, and is visible where a method reads the teacher \emph{off} the clean point. IGDM
\citep{igdm} matches a finite difference $f_T(x+\delta)-f_T(x-\delta)$ as a stand-in for
the teacher's input gradient, which is a gradient only while the teacher is locally linear; its own
diagnostic reports a Taylor remainder proportion of $0.012$ for adversarially trained teachers.
Applying that diagnostic to our checkpoints gives $0.0111$ for an adversarially trained
ResNet-18---reproducing their value, so the measurement is calibrated---and $\mathbf{0.4763}$ for the
natural teacher, forty times further from linear. The target it constructs is correspondingly
degenerate: $\lVert f_T(x+\delta)-f_T(x-\delta)\rVert=14.14$ against $\lVert f_T(x)\rVert=18.36$, with
the softmax of that difference placing $0.917$ on a single class.

The teacher therefore holds accuracy that adversarial training gives up, and the logit route does not
recover it. What follows keeps the read point at $x$, where the first defect cannot arise, and takes
the features, which the isolation above shows to be the better read.

\subsection{What a Clean-Feature Anchor Controls}
\label{sec:decomp}

Consider matching the teacher's feature at the \emph{clean} input against the student's feature at the
\emph{adversarial} input, that is, minimizing
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(x) \;=\; \max_{x' \in B(x,\epsilon)} \big\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert^2 .
\label{eqn:anchor_percell}
\end{equation}
Read literally, \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} asks the student to reproduce a \emph{fixed} vector from
every point of the ball. That is a fidelity constraint, and a fidelity constraint alone would have no
reason to produce robustness. It turns out not to be one, and the reason is visible once the quantity
is compared with the two properties one would otherwise have to impose separately.

Fix $x$, write $B = B(x,\epsilon)$, and define
\begin{align}
L &= \max_{x' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:L}\\
F &= \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert , \label{eqn:F}\\
O &= \max_{x', x'' \in B} \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert . \label{eqn:O}
\end{align}
Here $L$ is the square root of \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell}, the quantity the method minimizes; $F$ is
\emph{fidelity}, how far the student is from the teacher at the clean point; and $O$ is
\emph{oscillation}, the diameter of the student's own feature map over the ball. $F$ is what
adversarial training gives up relative to natural training, and $O$ is what its label term buys. They
are ordinarily two objectives.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decomp}
For every $x$ and every $\epsilon \ge 0$,
\begin{equation}
L \;\le\; F + O \;\le\; 3L .
\label{eqn:decomp}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the right-hand inequality, $F \le L$ because $x \in B$, so the maximum in \Cref{eqn:L} is at least
the value at $x$. For $O$, insert $\Phi_t(x)$ between the two student features: for any
$x', x'' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_t(x) - \Phi_s(x'') \rVert
\;\le\; 2L ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x', x''$ gives $O \le 2L$. Adding the two bounds gives $F + O \le 3L$.
For the left-hand inequality, insert $\Phi_s(x)$ instead: for any $x' \in B$,
\begin{equation*}
\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; \lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_s(x) \rVert + \lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert
\;\le\; O + F ,
\end{equation*}
and taking the maximum over $x'$ gives $L \le F + O$.
\end{proof}

Only the triangle inequality is used, so \Cref{prop:decomp} holds for the networks we actually train:
no smoothness, no linearity, no assumption on the architecture or on the attack that realizes the
maximum. Two consequences follow immediately.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:both}
If the anchor is driven to $L \le \delta$ at $x$, then $F \le \delta$ and $O \le 2\delta$ at $x$.
\end{corollary}

That is the operative statement: driving one scalar down bounds \emph{both} properties, at rates that
differ by a factor of two and by nothing else. Conversely, by the left inequality, $L$ cannot be small
unless both are, so the objective admits no solution that satisfies one at the expense of the other.
A weighted sum $F + \lambda O$ has no such property for any fixed $\lambda$: its level sets contain
points where either term is arbitrarily large. This is why the anchor is stated as the whole objective
rather than as a regularizer, and why there is no coefficient to select.

\paragraph{Where the constant $3$ comes from, and how tight it is.} The factor is $1 + 2$: one from
$F \le L$ and two from $O \le 2L$, and the second is attained when the student's outputs at the two
extreme points of the ball sit on opposite sides of $\Phi_t(x)$ at distance $L$ each. Our trained
students are far from that configuration, which is the content of the measurement below.

\paragraph{The teacher appears at $x$ and nowhere else.} \Cref{eqn:anchor_percell} evaluates $\Phi_t$
at the clean point only, in the loss and in the attack that realizes the maximum. Whatever the teacher
does inside $B$ is therefore absent from the objective, and the instability measured in
\Cref{sec:fragile}---$63.8^\circ$ of rotation, $\times 2.45$ of norm---has no path into the
optimization. The same fact settles the symmetric question: since the teacher is never asked to resist
a perturbation, it cannot supply robustness either, and all of it is produced by the student's inner
maximization. \Cref{sec:components} tests this by moving the read point and changing nothing else.

\paragraph{Verification.} \Cref{prop:decomp} predicts that a student trained on $L$ ends up with a
small $O$, and the prediction is checkable on the trained network. Measured over $\epsilon = 8/255$
balls on CIFAR-100, $L = 7.58$, $F = 6.30$ and $O_{\mathrm{lb}} = 2.16$, against the teacher's own
$O = 6.68$: the student is $3.1\times$ more stable than the network it was distilled from, on the same
balls. Two things follow. The teacher's instability was not inherited, which is what the construction
predicts. And $F \gg O_{\mathrm{lb}}$, so what remains in the loss at convergence is fidelity rather
than instability---the anchor is not being satisfied by making the student flat.

\paragraph{What \Cref{prop:decomp} does not establish.} It bounds $F + O$, and $O$ is not a sufficient
statistic for robust accuracy: across the teacher ladder of \Cref{sec:geometry} it correlates with
AutoAttack at $r = +0.83$, the wrong sign. The proposition explains why anchoring to a non-robust
teacher is \emph{free}; it is not a robustness bound and we do not present it as one.
\Cref{app:robustness} gives two partial results on the complementary question, each with the reason it
falls short.
