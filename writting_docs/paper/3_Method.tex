\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Clean Feature Anchoring}
\label{sec:anchor}

Adversarial training requires the model to be confidently correct at every point of a ball around every
training example, and that requirement is the source of its cost in standard accuracy.
Methods that address the trade-off keep the requirement and soften the target it is imposed on, whereas
we replace the target itself.
The backbone is trained against a single fixed vector, namely the feature that a naturally trained
network of the same architecture assigns to the clean image.
The perturbation ball is then asked to map onto that vector rather than onto a confident label.
The network supplying the vector is not robust, which is a deliberate choice: it provides a target that
is accurate, and the robustness is produced by the maximization that finds $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$.
Such a division of labour is only coherent once the label loss is removed, because a label loss and an
inaccurate-but-robust signal would otherwise compete for the same parameters.
The remainder of this subsection states the objective and three properties that follow from its form
rather than from measurement, and \Cref{sec:angeps} treats the one quantity that is left to choose,
namely how large a ball each example receives.

Let $\Phi_t$ be the frozen feature map of a network of the same architecture trained naturally on the
same data, and let $h_t$ be its classifier. The student's feature map is initialized at
$\Phi_s \leftarrow \Phi_t$ and trained with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_x \big\lVert \Phi_s(x_{\mathrm{adv}}) - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert^2 ,
\qquad
x_{\mathrm{adv}} \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x' \in B(x,\epsilon)}
  \big\lVert \Phi_s(x') - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert ,
\label{eqn:anchor}
\end{equation}
where the inner maximization is realized by $K$ steps of projected gradient ascent on the same
quantity the outer problem minimizes,
\begin{equation}
x^{(k+1)} \;=\; \Pi_{B(x,\epsilon)}\!\left(
  x^{(k)} + a \cdot \mathrm{sign}\, \nabla_{x}
  \big\lVert \Phi_s(x^{(k)}) - \Phi_t(x) \big\rVert^2 \right),
\qquad x^{(0)} = x + \eta ,
\label{eqn:pgd}
\end{equation}
with $\eta$ a small random initialization and $\Pi$ the projection onto the ball.
The target $\Phi_t(x)$ is a constant of this ascent, since it is computed once at the clean input and
does not move as $x^{(k)}$ does.
The inner and outer problems therefore optimize the same quantity, as they do in standard adversarial
training and unlike methods whose attack is generated against a different objective from the one being
trained.
\Cref{eqn:anchor} is the entire objective for the backbone, without a label term, a logit term or a
coefficient joining them, and the three properties below are consequences of that form.

\paragraph{(i) The teacher never enters the perturbation ball.}
The teacher is evaluated at $x$ alone in both \Cref{eqn:anchor} and \Cref{eqn:pgd}, so the objective
depends on $\Phi_t$ only through the single vector $\Phi_t(x)$.
Two teachers that agree at $x$ and differ arbitrarily on $B(x,\epsilon) \setminus \{x\}$ consequently
induce the same optimization problem, and the rotation that the teacher undergoes under attack
(\Cref{sec:analysis}) is exactly such a difference.
The same argument runs in the other direction as well, because a quantity the objective cannot observe
cannot serve as a source of robustness either.
The restriction is load-bearing rather than convenient.
Evaluating $\Phi_t$ at $x'$ instead would make $\Phi_s = \Phi_t$ a global minimizer of
\Cref{eqn:anchor}, and that minimizer is a natural model; the student trained this way collapses onto
the teacher and reaches $0.00$ AutoAttack accuracy (\Cref{tab:controls}).
Restricting the teacher to $x$ therefore keeps the fragility of the teacher out of the objective and
keeps the objective from admitting a degenerate solution.

\paragraph{(ii) The anchor is exactly zero at the clean point at initialization.}
The initialization $\Phi_s \leftarrow \Phi_t$ gives $\lVert \Phi_s(x) - \Phi_t(x) \rVert = 0$ for every
$x$, so the loss is nonzero only because of the perturbation.
Every gradient the backbone receives at the start of training is thus attributable to the attack, which
is the quantity we intend to train against.
A logit target has the same property only at temperature $1$, where it carries no dark knowledge.
The property is also used by \Cref{sec:angeps}: the per-sample sensitivities are identical at the first
step, so the radius allocation begins uniform and differentiates only as the student moves away from
its teacher.

\paragraph{(iii) The classifier is inherited, which is what a feature target buys.}
The student at test time is $h_t \circ \Phi_s$, and the head is never trained.
Inheriting the head is possible because the target is written on the feature.
A loss written on logits constrains only the composition $W\Phi$, and it therefore determines the
pair $(\Phi, W)$ only up to the gauge
\begin{equation}
(\Phi, W) \;\longmapsto\; (A\Phi,\; W A^{-1}), \qquad A \in \mathrm{GL}(d),
\label{eqn:gauge}
\end{equation}
so that the same logits are reachable from a continuum of representations, none of which the teacher's
classifier is calibrated for.
Fixing the target at the feature removes this freedom and pins $\Phi_s$ to $\Phi_t$ itself, which is
what keeps $h_t$ the correct classifier for it.
\Cref{tab:ablation} shows that inheriting the head is preferred rather than merely permissible, since
every alternative we tried scores below leaving the classifier alone, including the best head
temperature.
Inheritance also removes the last two hyperparameters, as a head distillation term would reintroduce
both a temperature and a weight.

\subsection{Sensitivity-Matched $\epsilon$}
\label{sec:angeps}

\paragraph{The problem a uniform radius creates.}
\Cref{eqn:anchor} is optimized over a pixel ball while the quantity it measures is a feature
displacement, and the two are not proportional.
A single $\epsilon$ therefore moves the objective by an amount that varies substantially across the
dataset, and the coefficient of variation of $\lVert \nabla_x \mathcal{L} \rVert$ within a minibatch is
$0.64$ when logged over training.
Examples that receive the same pixel budget are consequently attacked with very different force in the
geometry the loss is written in, which is not something \Cref{eqn:anchor} asks for.

\paragraph{Equalizing the movement.}
We assign the radius per sample in order to equalize how far the attack moves the objective rather than
how far it moves the input.
The first-order change of a differentiable loss inside an $\ell_\infty$ ball of radius $e$ is
\begin{equation}
\max_{\lVert \delta \rVert_\infty \le e} \langle \nabla_x \mathcal{L}, \delta \rangle
\;=\; e \, \lVert \nabla_x \mathcal{L} \rVert_1 ,
\label{eqn:firstorder}
\end{equation}
since $\ell_1$ is the dual norm of $\ell_\infty$.
Writing $g_i = \lVert \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x_i) \rVert_1$, the movement induced at example $i$ by a
radius $\epsilon_i$ is $\epsilon_i g_i$ to first order.
Equalizing that movement across a minibatch of $N$ examples amounts to imposing
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_i \, g_i \;=\; c \quad \text{for all } i ,
\qquad \text{subject to} \qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{N} \epsilon_i \;=\; N \epsilon .
\label{eqn:equalize}
\end{equation}
The constraint holds the total attack budget fixed, which is what makes the rule comparable to a
uniform radius at equal attack strength.
Solving \Cref{eqn:equalize} gives $c = N\epsilon \big/ \sum_j g_j^{-1}$ and hence
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_i \;=\; N\epsilon \cdot \frac{g_i^{-1}}{\sum_j g_j^{-1}} ,
\qquad\text{generalized to}\qquad
\epsilon_i \;\propto\; g_i^{-p} ,
\label{eqn:angeps_ideal}
\end{equation}
where $p = 0$ recovers the uniform radius and $p = 1$ gives the full equalization above.
The allocation of \Cref{eqn:angeps_ideal} is equivalently the max--min solution, because moving budget
from the example whose objective moves most to the example whose objective moves least improves the
worst case until no such move remains.
Our implementation reads the gradient in $\ell_2$ rather than in the $\ell_1$ derived here, for the
reason given in \Cref{app:box}, and the two norms differ by $0.29$ points of AutoAttack accuracy.
What the rule gains over a uniform radius is preserved in direction under either norm and is larger on
the clean axis than on the robust one, and \Cref{app:box} reports both settings.

\paragraph{Keeping the radii in range.}
\Cref{eqn:angeps_ideal} assigns unbounded radii where $g_i \to 0$, which is the situation at the start
of training by property (ii).
We therefore constrain $\epsilon_i / \epsilon \in [0.5, 1.5]$ and solve the budget constraint inside
that box, which \Cref{app:box} states exactly and which reduces to restoring the mean when no clip is
active.
We use $p = 1$ throughout and never tune it, and \Cref{tab:allocation} reports the $p = 0$ endpoint
under the same recipe.

\paragraph{Relation to existing per-sample radii.}
Assigning a different $\epsilon$ to each example is not new, as IAAT \citep{iaat}, MMA \citep{mma} and
CAT \citep{cat} all do so.
These methods assign the radius from the difficulty of the example or from its margin in input space,
whereas \Cref{eqn:angeps_ideal} assigns it from the input sensitivity of the training loss, which is
the geometry the objective is written in.
The difference is not one of dispersion, since all three signals are dispersed on the shipped recipe
and ours is in fact the least dispersed of them (\Cref{app:box}).
What separates the rules is the geometry along which the budget is spread, and the experiments in
\Cref{sec:exp} therefore compare competing allocations rather than an allocation against its
absence.%

The distinction can be tested without confounding it with the budget.
We hold the exact multiset of weights that \Cref{eqn:angeps_ideal} produces, so that the values, the
clip and the mean are unchanged, and only reassign which example receives which weight in the order
that IAAT and CAT would use.
The reassignment alone costs $0.80$ points of AutoAttack accuracy and falls below the uniform
baseline on AutoAttack, CW and NRR alike.
Replacing the signal outright by difficulty magnitude or by the logit margin of MMA is likewise worse
than allocating nothing (\Cref{tab:allocation}).
Among the signals we tested, only the input sensitivity of the loss improves on a uniform radius.

\Cref{alg:cfa} states the procedure in full.
Training the natural teacher is ordinary supervised training and is the only place where labels
appear.
The anchored phase uses no labels at all, because the teacher vector $\phi_i$ read once per example at
the clean input is the entire supervisory signal, and the classifier is carried through untouched.
The method introduces no loss weight, no temperature and no auxiliary head, and the teacher checkpoint
is the only quantity we vary between datasets.

\paragraph{Caveats.}
\Cref{eqn:firstorder} is derived for the $\ell_1$ norm while our runs read the gradient in $\ell_2$,
and the gap between the two is small but larger than our seed spread, as reported in \Cref{app:box}.
The sensitivity $g_i$ is also a single backward pass at the clean $x_i$, so it measures the sensitivity
at the starting point of the attack rather than along its trajectory.
