\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Adversarial training remains the most reliable defense against norm-bounded attacks
\citep{PGD,athalye2018obfuscated,AutoAttack}, but its robustness comes with lower accuracy on clean
inputs. This trade-off is partly intrinsic to the training objective \citep{tsipras2018robustness,TRADES}
and becomes especially restrictive for compact networks. Existing approaches improve the target seen
during adversarial training: some replace one-hot labels with softened or self-distilled targets
\citep{pang2020bag,tack2022consistency,adr}, while adversarial distillation transfers predictions from
a robust teacher \citep{ard,iad,rslad,adaad,igdm}. The latter can be effective, but presupposes a model
that has already paid the cost of robust training.

A naturally trained copy of the student provides inexpensive clean guidance, despite being
non-robust itself. Previous methods exploit its predictions or representations
\citep{lbgat,arrest,dpfat,bmtard}, but choosing a target leaves two further decisions: which natural
checkpoint to use, and how strongly to perturb each training example. Neither follows directly
from the teacher's clean accuracy. We study these decisions through a simple feature-regression
objective, asking how the teacher and the perturbation budget shape the student's clean--robust
trade-off.

\textbf{Clean Feature Anchoring} (CFA) uses the natural teacher's clean feature as a fixed target
for both adversarial-example generation and backbone learning, while retaining its classifier.
This gives a single objective without a label-loss coefficient or temperature search during
adversarial training. A fixed-anchor bound characterizes its joint control of teacher fidelity
and local feature variation. We use this simple objective to examine teacher selection and
perturbation allocation.

With this objective fixed, a more accurate natural teacher need not produce a more accurate
student on clean inputs. Along the CIFAR-100 teacher trajectory we examine, teacher clean accuracy
increases by $2.51$ points, yet student clean accuracy falls by $2.04$ while robustness improves.
An earlier-versus-later checkpoint comparison on Tiny-ImageNet follows the same direction.
Thus teacher selection changes the student's trade-off rather than simply improving both axes.
The practical implication is to assess a teacher by the student it produces, not by its own clean
accuracy alone.

For a chosen teacher, a second mismatch remains: equal pixel-space radii can induce different
changes in the anchor loss across examples. We therefore allocate the training radius using the
input sensitivity of that same loss. Locally sensitive examples receive smaller radii and less
sensitive examples larger ones, with the batch total held fixed. An inverse-sensitivity rule is
motivated by equalizing the linearized loss increase. Importantly, we test the allocation signal
by reassigning exactly the same radius multipliers across examples, separating their assignment
from their distribution. Teacher selection and radius allocation thus address complementary
choices: the reference representation and the neighborhoods over which it is preserved.

CFA achieves competitive clean--robust trade-offs across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and
Tiny-ImageNet. On CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 it reaches $64.75\%$ clean and $27.98\%$ AutoAttack
accuracy (\Cref{tab:main}). Controls on label supervision, regression targets, optimization recipes,
and radius allocation distinguish the contribution of the anchor from that of the training stack.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We identify a teacher-selection trade-off: along the studied natural-training trajectory,
  higher teacher clean accuracy accompanies lower student clean accuracy and higher robustness,
  with the same direction observed in a Tiny-ImageNet checkpoint comparison.
  \item We develop sensitivity-based radius allocation for a single clean-feature objective,
  motivated by a fixed-budget first-order analysis and tested against controls that preserve
  the radius distribution.
  \item We evaluate CFA across three datasets and two architectures, and use target, recipe, and
  allocation controls to characterize where this simple natural-teacher training design is effective.
\end{itemize}
